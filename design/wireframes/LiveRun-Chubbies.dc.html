The $2.98 fee for a promise your product page already made

Charging for return coverage is a legitimate call — apparel returns run 20–30% and somebody has to pay for them. The problem isn't the fee. It's that your product page says “Easy returns & exchanges within 30 days” — and two clicks later the cart asks $2.98 for the same promise. Align the two and this moment starts working for you.

The fee arrives pre-selected, explained only by a tooltip, and the way out is a button that frames declining as losing something. It then reappears on the payment page as “Unlock free returns — $2.98.” Three asks, one contradiction.

WHAT YOUR CUSTOMER COMPARES YOU TO

Bonobos' site banner, checked this morning: “Free Shipping & Returns in the US.” One sentence, no asterisk, no fee.

Unexpected costs surfaced at checkout are the most-cited reason carts die — 48% of abandoning shoppers name them (Baymard, 2024). A pre-selected fee is the sharpest version of an unexpected cost: it was chosen for them.

The fix: make one promise and keep it everywhere.

A · Honest add-on — rename it “Return coverage (optional) — $2.98,” unchecked by default, with a neutral decline. The product page drops “easy returns” or defines it.

Five badges, one discount

On your Shorts grid, every product is $64.50 struck to $40.00 — the sitewide promo. But each card wears a different label: ALL-TIME BESTSELLER · PRICE DROP · MARKDOWN · ON DISCOUNT · SALE PRICE. Five names for the same $24.50.

ALL-TIME BESTSELLER$64.50 $40.00 PRICE DROP$64.50 $40.00 MARKDOWN$64.50 $40.00

Variety reads as merchandising energy — until a customer compares two cards, and the grid puts them side by side. The opportunity: when one label stops being literal, your true claims get discounted with it — the real bestseller, the genuinely low stock, the reviews. One honest label gives all of those their power back.

The fix: one honest label for the promo (“$40 & Under”), and save the special badges for claims that are individually true. Theme-level; an afternoon.
